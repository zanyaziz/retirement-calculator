Monte Carlo — what are the odds?

Tab 1 assumes your return is the same every year. Real markets aren’t. This tab runs thousands of different market histories using the same balance sheet and savings from Tab 1 — same starting net worth, same annual savings, same mean return — but each year’s return is drawn at random. The spread of outcomes is the honest answer: a median, a bad-luck path, a good-luck path, and your chance of clearing each percentile bar while that bar keeps rising.

How this works. The percentile bars are moving targets: in the simulation each bar rises at its documented long-run SCF rate — about 5.5%/yr for every rung from 1989 to 2025 (the 90th-percentile bar compounded at 5.52%/yr; the 95th and 99th at 5.59%/yr; the 93rd, 97th and 98th are assigned that same common pace, since they were only published in later surveys). Recent survey windows ran faster than the long run, which is what the bar-growth adjustment is for. Your percentile between published rungs is a log-linear interpolation — an estimate, not a measurement. The Tab 1 projection holds your income, savings, return, and appreciation constant, adds your savings to your invested assets each year, keeps liabilities flat, and ignores market swings and life changes. Tab 2 (Monte Carlo) relaxes that last part: returns vary randomly each year around the same mean, so you see a distribution and odds instead of a single line. Tab 3 (Retirement) takes the Tab 1 projection to your chosen retirement year, then draws the pool down against inflation-rising spending, less Social Security (estimated with the 2026 SSA bend-point formula on capped, level earnings) and any other income, until it runs out; the primary home marked in §3 is excluded from that pool unless you choose to count it. These are scenarios, not forecasts, and not financial advice. Your entries never leave this page — nothing is stored or sent anywhere.
